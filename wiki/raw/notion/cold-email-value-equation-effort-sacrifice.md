# 05 - Variable 4: Effort & Sacrifice
## How Hormozi frames it
<callout icon="ℹ️">
	*"Marketing agency A: three meetings a week, four hours to deliver us copy and creative. The other agency: we've already tested this stuff for your industry, we've got videographers, we can just start running the stuff - you just pass it off and it's taken care of. How much more valuable is the guy who takes none of your effort and sacrifice to make this outcome occur?"*
</callout>
\>
<callout icon="ℹ️">
	- Alex Hormozi
</callout>
Effort and sacrifice is how much work the prospect has to do to get the result.
It's the simplest variable to understand conceptually, and it's the one where most cold emails leak the most value. Every task you imply on the prospect's side adds friction. Every task you move onto *your* side removes friction. The goal is not to be honest and upfront about what they'll have to do - the goal is to make the outcome feel as close to effortless as possible inside the offer.
---
## The common mistake
Spending paragraphs on the process. Who does what. How the client will collaborate. What tools will be onboarded. What meetings will be held.
<callout icon="ℹ️">
	*"We'll schedule a kickoff, then assign your account manager, then onboard your team to our dashboard, then run weekly syncs and monthly reviews…"*
</callout>
All that does is pile implied work onto the prospect. They start mentally sorting: *do I have time for kickoffs? do I want another dashboard to log into? is my team going to resist another tool? who on my side owns this?* The hesitation piles up. Hesitation is functionally a no.
The point of addressing effort in a cold email isn't to be as transparent as possible. It's to make the outcome look as effortless as possible.
---
## The 2026 way
Move every task you possibly can **onto your side of the line** - and say so explicitly.
The sentence structure that does most of the work:
<callout icon="ℹ️">
	*"We'll \[do 4-5 specific things\]. All you have to do is \[one small, clearly-bounded thing\]."*
</callout>
The 4-5 tasks on your side show competence and remove ambiguity about scope. The one task on their side shows how small the lift is. Together, the sentence does more for your reply rate than most paragraphs of copy.
You're not asking them to restructure their business. You're asking them for 20 minutes. The email should feel that small.
---
## Real script: continuing the YouTube example
<callout icon="ℹ️">
	*We'd handle the ideation, the script, the editing, and the thumbnail. All you have to do is record - 20 to 30 minutes on camera and we take it from there.*
</callout>
One sentence. It does all of the following:
- Lists four specific tasks on your side (proof of capability)
- Reduces the prospect's entire involvement to a single action (record)
- Quantifies the time commitment (20-30 minutes)
- Removes every implied task - no kickoff call, no onboarding process, no tools to learn, no team alignment meetings
The prospect finishes that sentence and knows exactly what they'd be signing up for. There's nothing left up in the air, which means there's nothing left to object to.
---
## The "All you have to do is" template
Rewrite your effort section using this exact structure:
<callout icon="ℹ️">
	**Your side:** \{3-5 specific tasks you handle end-to-end\}
</callout>
\>
<callout icon="ℹ️">
	**Their side:** \{one clearly-bounded action\} \{+ time window if useful\}
</callout>

If a task genuinely has to live on their side, make sure there's only one of them and bound it with a time estimate.

## The multiplier effect
Effort doesn't just reduce friction on its own. When stacked with the other three variables, it compounds.
- **Effort + Time Delay.** *"We'll have your first video live in 7 days. All you do is record for 20 minutes."* Fast outcome AND almost nothing on their side. This combination is almost impossible to reject.
- **Effort + Perceived Likelihood.** Listing the 4 specific tasks you handle is itself proof you've done this before. Specificity reads as capability.
- **Effort + Dream Outcome.** Lowering the implied effort makes even a big dream outcome feel closer to achievable. The prospect goes from *"that sounds like a lot"* to *"wait, what am I actually being asked to do here?"*
When all four variables are dialed in together, the offer doesn't read like an offer anymore. It reads like a small favor that happens to move them toward something they want.

## Checklist before sending
- [ ] Is there a single sentence showing 3-5 specific things you handle?
- [ ] Is the prospect's total involvement a single, clearly-bounded action?
- [ ] Have you quantified their time commitment in minutes or hours?
- [ ] Have you deleted every implied task - syncs, onboarding, tools, reporting, reviews?
