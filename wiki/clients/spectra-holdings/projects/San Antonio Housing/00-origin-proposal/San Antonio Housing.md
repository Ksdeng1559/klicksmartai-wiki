# Housing Delivery and Job Creation Strategy

## 1. Operating Frame

This plan outlines Spectra’s Housing Delivery and Job Creation Strategy in the City of San Antonio. The strategy is designed to build the first 5,000 single-family and multi-family units within San Antonio city limits over a five-year period.

The strategy focuses on Spectra’s four pillars:

1. Health
2. Housing
3. Economics
4. Education

These pillars are supported by innovation and technology. Homelessness and Continuum of Care metrics may be referenced as supporting context, but the execution geography, siting, and delivery commitments are strictly within San Antonio city limits.

---

## 2. Demographics

San Antonio is one of the nation’s fastest-growing large cities, but it also experiences persistent poverty and sharp neighborhood-level disparities. City reporting reflects that poverty has remained largely stagnant over the last decade at approximately one in five residents. San Antonio remains economically segregated, with pockets of poverty inside city limits.

This matters for delivery because the strategy must be geographically precise. Spectra must target census tracts and neighborhoods where attainable homeownership, workforce housing, and stabilized monthly costs will produce the largest measurable impact.

- U.S. Census Bureau, American Community Survey 1-year estimates, 2024.
- U.S. Census Bureau, American Community Survey 5-year estimates, 2024.

## 3. Strategy Overview

This strategy places heavy emphasis on technology in Spectra’s homes to make them more cost effective to maintain, reduce utility volatility, and reduce the long-term hidden costs that typically destabilize low-to-moderate income households, including maintenance shocks, energy cost spikes, and deferred repairs.

Spectra’s homes are designed to be disaster-resistant and energy-efficient, with intelligent-home readiness that supports durability, safety, and predictable operating costs.

This strategy is paired with a community wealth-building model that keeps value circulating locally. Spectra’s vertical integration in manufacturing, distribution, design, construction, and training is used to compress cycle time, reduce waste, and stabilize quality.

The intended outcome is a repeatable production system that can scale across multiple sites inside San Antonio while maintaining strict code compliance and consistent construction standards.

---

## 4. Housing Need and Affordability Reality

San Antonio’s core constraint is not demand. It is deliverable supply at price points that working households can sustain.

City reporting indicates poverty remains materially higher than the national average, and that educational attainment and income disparities correlate with housing instability. City data also reflects that a large share of renter households are cost-burdened, meaning they spend more than 30% of income on housing. The city’s affordability challenge varies dramatically by neighborhood.

The practical implication is that affordability must be defined as reduced monthly cost of living, not only purchase price. Reduced mortgages, reduced energy costs, and reduced maintenance exposure are the levers that convert a unit into a stable household outcome.

---

## 5. Target Populations and Unit Mix

This strategy serves four primary resident segments inside the City:

1. Workforce households, including teachers, first responders, healthcare workers, skilled trades, and municipal employees.
2. Low-to-moderate income households at 10% to 80% AMI that are currently cost-burdened and at risk of displacement, including those on waiting lists for housing and with terminal illnesses.
3. Seniors on fixed incomes needing stable monthly housing costs and low maintenance.
4. Families transitioning out of shelter or unstable housing when paired with appropriate support partners and fully funded operations.

### Unit Mix

- Single-family detached homes as the primary wealth-building product.
- Multifamily units where density is required, with a defined pathway to condominium conversion when feasible to transition households from renter to owner without leaving their community.
- Limited supportive or transition units delivered through partners only when the operating model is fully funded, including services, reserves, and long-term operating controls.

## 6. Site Strategy

Spectra will pursue a three-lane site pipeline so production is not bottlenecked by any one land type. The pipeline is designed to produce early wins quickly while building a scalable base of subdivision and mixed-use production.

### 6.1 Lane A — Infill Clusters

Infill clusters are small groups of lots that can be permitted and started quickly. This lane is used to demonstrate early wins, establish inspection rhythm, and place families into ownership rapidly.

Execution steps:

- identify target neighborhoods with existing utilities and clear title;
- standardize a small set of plan types for repeat permitting;
- batch procurement and scheduling to reduce per-unit overhead.

### 6.2 Lane B — Subdivision-Scale Tracts

Subdivision tracts provide the volume required to materially affect supply and pricing. Spectra will prioritize partnerships with landowners that include a profit-sharing model.

This structure can create tax advantages for the landowner and can generate two to three times the profit opportunity relative to the property’s current value when executed under a coordinated entitlement and vertical construction plan.

Landowners are treated as co-developers by contributing land value into the project structure. This lowers Spectra’s upfront costs, accelerates site control, and supports faster delivery at attainable price points. Landowner profit participation is realized at closing under the program’s pay-as-we-sell distribution mechanics.

Execution steps:

- secure tract control through landowner partnership and profit-sharing programs;
- complete entitlement and engineering with City coordination;
- phase horizontal and vertical construction to maintain continuous closings.

### 6.3 Lane C — Mixed-Use Nodes

Mixed-use nodes combine housing with community-serving retail and services to reduce household transportation burden and support local job creation.

## 7. Capacity Strategy

Housing delivery at scale must be matched to capacity. Spectra will not advance sites strictly based on land availability. Every site must be screened for capacity constraints and upgrade requirements, including:

- school capacity and enrollment pressure, coordinated with ISDs and City / County partners;
- water and wastewater capacity, lift stations, and treatment constraints;
- drainage and flood mitigation requirements;
- roadway access, traffic impacts, and emergency response access;
- power and broadband readiness where required for technology-enabled housing systems.

Spectra will coordinate with the City and County to make determinations based on current infrastructure conditions and to jointly identify funding pathways for upgrades. This includes aligning infrastructure projects with economic development incentives, public infrastructure funding, and performance-based tools so that infrastructure becomes a catalyst for housing starts rather than a bottleneck.

## 8. Delivery System

Spectra’s delivery system is built around vertical integration, repeatable production, and disciplined local execution.

### 8.1 Design-to-Permit Standardization

Spectra will establish a permit-ready plan set approved for repeat use, create a City-facing submittal checklist and review cadence, and maintain a rolling queue of permit packages so crews never idle.

### 8.2 Manufacturing and Supply Chain Control

Spectra will lock critical materials and components into controlled procurement, use batch scheduling to reduce price volatility, and maintain quality control checkpoints tied to inspection stages.

### 8.3 Construction Operations and Workforce

Spectra will hire from the City itself in supply chain, manufacturing, and construction roles, supported by skilled and quality training from the local workforce ecosystem.

Spectra will depend upon dedicated workers from the community. This is not a secondary benefit; it is a core operational requirement. Production volume, quality consistency, and code-compliant delivery depend on stable local labor, disciplined site supervision, and structured training pathways that create upward mobility for residents while building the workforce needed to deliver 5,000 units.

### 8.4 Ultimate State Management Strategy

Spectra will appoint an Executive Vice President over the entire State of Texas who coordinates with the Company’s C-level team and Corporate Office to initiate programs in a particular area, mobilize capital, and align municipal relationships.

Spectra will then appoint a General Contractor from the area who will manage all projects and employees in that county, including all local subcontractors. This structure creates complete project dedication and local talent that is familiar with local codes, inspection practices, and supply networks.

This is the ultimate production strategy because it scales volume without sacrificing compliance, schedule control, or quality.

## 9. Capital Strategy

This strategy is designed to remain executable even when individual program cycles pause, while still leveraging City tools where they accelerate starts.

### 9.1 Master Credit Facility as the Scaling Engine and Capital Recycling

Spectra’s Master Credit Facility model is structured as a senior secured revolving facility deployed into multiple SPE subsidiaries, each with separate legal status, accounting, and collateral packages.

The facility is designed to support simultaneous construction capacity. As units sell, proceeds are applied to pay accrued interest and return principal to a recycling pool for redeployment into subsequent batches.

This capital recycling approach is fundamentally distinct from project-by-project debt and is intended to support cumulative deployment materially in excess of the initial commitment over the facility term.

The MCF structure supports continuous starts and continuous closings, with unit-level tracking and reporting.

### 9.2 City Participation

City support is requested primarily in the form of process alignment and infrastructure coordination. Projects must be approved in a timely manner to initiate additional investment from investors and support tax credit execution for institutional tax credit purchases where applicable.

Spectra requests:

- fast-track approvals for repeat plan sets;
- phased subdivisions;
- standardized inspections;
- fee delays;
- potentially no or deferred impact fees where feasible, tied to measurable affordability, job creation, and neighborhood stabilization outcomes.

City participation may also include City-owned property contributions where appropriate to reduce upfront costs and accelerate delivery.

## 10. Governance, Compliance, and Risk Controls

Spectra will operate with a compliance-first posture suitable for municipal partnership. Spectra and others that do business with Spectra will ensure obedience to all local, state, and federal codes and regulations.

It is imperative that the City have strict code compliance rules, and Spectra’s model is designed to support those rules as a neighborhood stabilization mechanism.

Spectra replaces a traditional homeowner’s association with a management system that depends on new homeowners obeying local codes and ordinances. Spectra will reinforce compliance through education, documented standards, and accountability mechanisms that protect property condition, neighborhood quality, and long-run affordability.

### 10.1 Developer’s Credit and Mortgage Reduction

Spectra will provide new homeowners with a Developer’s Credit, where Spectra provides cash to the purchase not as a concession, but as a cash deposit derived from grants, donations, subsidies, and other contributions to reduce the mortgage of the homes.

Spectra will provide the credit based on certain criteria in replacement of credit worthiness, emphasizing verified readiness and stability. Requirements include:

1. A Spectra-approved financial literacy course from partners.
2. If applicable, a workforce course for job training or vocational school.
3. A Spectra-approved home maintenance course describing rules, careful upkeep, and code compliance expectations.
4. A Spectra-approved homebuying course that teaches contracts and home purchasing.

After completing these courses, residents receive points for successful completion and may be awarded up to $200,000 credit from company reserves to reduce their mortgage based on income and completion thresholds.

This credit is unique because Spectra is the manufacturer, distributor of materials, builder, operator, and developer. Excess cash and profits are routed into controlled accounts that substantially fund down payments and lower the mortgage or LTV for the homes.

Spectra does not lose money; funds come to the company in many forms, and prior to processing, Spectra applies them toward down payments. In theory, this is like moving money from the left pocket to the right pocket, but with the practical outcome of materially reduced mortgages for residents.

These mortgages are the key to actual affordability. Homeownership drives down the cost of living when the mortgage is reduced and operating costs are stabilized through resilient construction and technology-driven efficiency.

### 10.2 Pay-As-We-Sell Distributions

Spectra’s mission is to remain debt-free at the corporate level by leveraging debt that is paid through a pay-as-we-sell strategy.

This allows landowners who contributed land to get paid profit sharing at closing, and it allows lenders, investors, institutions, and government agencies to receive their defined distributions at closing.

The homeowner receives a new home with a reduced mortgage supported by a partner and later a subsidiary of Spectra. Spectra’s development firm and subsidiaries retain substantial profits through this community wealth-building model.

## 11. Cost-of-Living Reduction Ecosystem

To further drive down the cost of living, residents in Spectra’s ecosystem and their families will become members of a Spectra community membership program. This membership will allow participants to shop and receive special discounts and offers from Spectra commercial brands and participating partner small businesses.

The purpose is to:

- lower the cost of living;
- create community wealth;
- drive targeted job creation;
- accelerate small business growth;
- circulate wealth in a specific community.

Spectra plans to build a commercial district in San Antonio that will include multiple Spectra brands, partner brands, and unique local brands. The commercial district is not an add-on. It is a cost-of-living strategy tied to Spectra’s four pillars.

Spectra also plans to build an education campus with vocational training, contractor training, and charter school pathways from K–12. This will be structured as a hybrid model consisting of nonprofit and for-profit entities where appropriate.

Spectra’s medical campus will include a healthcare program with specialized doctors, surgeons, vision, dental, urgent care, and inpatient / outpatient services through a nonprofit model. The purpose is to make care more affordable and keep proceeds circulating through the organization rather than extracting value through shareholders in service delivery.

This model is intended to serve low-to-moderate income families and residents in the ecosystem while accepting Medicare, Medicaid, and insurance products.

Spectra also intends to develop a nonprofit organic grocery store concept with no shareholders, designed to lower the cost of healthy groceries and prepared foods, potentially supplied through Spectra’s agricultural division and organic producer partners.

## 13. Relevant San Antonio Programs and Targeting Tools

### 13.1 HIP 120 Down Payment Assistance

The City’s HIP 120 materials indicate a defined process that includes pre-qualification for a first lien mortgage, completion of a City eligibility form, and completion of a HUD-approved homebuyer education class.

The program also states the home must be located within San Antonio city limits and includes a minimum earnest money deposit requirement.

Spectra can align its homebuyer readiness pathway, including financial literacy, homebuyer education, and maintenance courses, to dovetail with City requirements where the program is available and open for funding cycles.

### 13.2 SHIP — Strategic Housing Implementation Plan

The SHIP document reflects a City and county-aligned housing strategy framework, with adopting partners including Bexar County, the City of San Antonio, SAHA, and SAHT.

SHIP defines affordability to include renter income restrictions up to 60% AMI and homeowner income up to 120% AMI, as defined in the plan’s affordability framework.

SHIP also emphasizes that meeting need requires multiple tools:

- producing and preserving homes;
- raising incomes;
- using job training and other stabilizing programs.

Spectra’s strategy aligns with this framework by combining production with workforce training, cost-of-living reduction, and resident readiness systems that reduce default risk and neighborhood decline.

### 13.3 Census Tract Targeting

The San Antonio census tract document provides a tract-level income designation framework for low, moderate, middle, and upper assessment areas.

This can be used to prioritize Lane A and Lane B site selection and to align affordability commitments, incentive routing, and community benefits to the tracts with the highest need and strongest eligibility alignment for public tools.

## 15. Comprehensive Development Plan — SA Tomorrow Alignment

Spectra’s San Antonio strategy is designed to function as an implementation partner to the City’s Comprehensive Development Plan, not as a parallel plan.

The Growth and City Form element establishes the City’s policy intent:

- manage growth in a way that protects historic neighborhoods and cultural assets;
- improve livability and public health;
- increase the City’s ability to capture regional housing demand within city limits rather than losing market share to unincorporated Bexar County.

Spectra’s delivery model is structured to convert that intent into measurable unit production and stabilized monthly housing costs while maintaining strict compliance with zoning, infrastructure capacity, environmental constraints, and neighborhood context.

### 15.1 Priority Growth Areas, Regional Centers, and Corridors

The Comprehensive Development Plan calls for higher-density uses to be focused within the City’s regional centers and along arterial and transit corridors, and for priority growth areas to attract both jobs and residents.

Spectra’s site pipeline is therefore organized to prioritize locations where the City already intends to concentrate intensity and infrastructure investment:

- regional centers;
- major arterials;
- transit corridors;
- identified priority growth areas.

This supports the City’s competitiveness goals by increasing the supply of in-city housing options so household growth is captured within San Antonio rather than displaced outward.

### 15.2 Infill, Revitalization, and Mixed-Use Conversion

The Comprehensive Development Plan recognizes that San Antonio does not have sufficient residentially-zoned land to accommodate forecasted demand in several areas under current density patterns. It identifies conversion of vacant and underutilized commercial and industrial parcels, particularly in the urban core and along primary corridors, as a major pathway to increase housing supply and walkability.

Spectra’s Lane A, Infill Clusters, and Lane C, Mixed-Use Nodes, are designed to operationalize that directive by assembling permit-ready infill sites and targeting underutilized parcels for residentially-focused mixed-use development where appropriate.

### 15.3 Walkability, Livability, and Health Outcomes

The Comprehensive Development Plan links growth management to walkability, bikeability, neighborhood livability, and community health and wellness.

Spectra treats these outcomes as design and siting requirements rather than aspirational language. Projects are prioritized where residents can realistically access employment, retail, medical services, and recreation with reduced transportation burden, and where infrastructure coordination can support compact development patterns.

### 15.4 Affordable Housing, Segregation, and Mobility

The Comprehensive Development Plan identifies significant income inequality and economic segregation. It emphasizes the need to address affordable housing gaps while proactively mitigating displacement pressures near Downtown and along transit corridors.

Spectra’s housing mix and placement strategy is structured to avoid concentrating low-income households only in the lowest-cost geographies. The strategy supports a citywide distribution of housing types and affordability levels, including pathways to ownership where feasible, while preserving affordability within revitalizing neighborhoods and along transit corridors.

### 15.5 Capturing Regional Growth Inside City Limits

The Comprehensive Development Plan notes that the City’s capture of regional household growth has declined over time and that outward expansion is less fiscally beneficial than infill development.

Spectra’s city-limits execution frame is designed to address that dynamic by increasing the supply of in-city single-family and multifamily options, reducing pressure that pushes households outward, and supporting compact development patterns that improve long-term fiscal performance.

### 15.6 Environmental Sustainability, Water Conservation, and Stormwater Management

The Comprehensive Development Plan’s natural resource and environmental sustainability policies are treated as non-negotiable constraints in Spectra’s site selection and design standards.

These include:

- water conservation;
- stormwater best practices;
- protection of waterways;
- safeguards for the Edwards Aquifer.

Projects will be evaluated for compatibility with low-impact development techniques, pervious area requirements where applicable, and watershed master plan recommendations.

### 15.7 Education Access and Neighborhood Stability

The Comprehensive Development Plan explicitly links growth and city form to educational access and performance, including support for existing schools and the promotion of innovative educational opportunities within priority growth areas to drive market demand for housing.

Spectra’s four-pillar model is structured to reinforce this linkage by coordinating housing delivery with workforce training, neighborhood stabilization, and partnerships that support educational access in growth areas where appropriate.

## 16. Placement and Replacement Strategy

### 16.1 Placement Strategy — Vacant Land

Spectra’s placement strategy is governed by its Buy–Partner–Build–Sell execution model and is designed to build on as much vacant land as possible within San Antonio city limits.

The objective is to convert dormant and underutilized parcels into productive housing supply by strategically placing Spectra’s disaster-resistant and energy-efficient homes where infrastructure access, entitlement feasibility, and neighborhood compatibility support responsible delivery.

Spectra’s delivery posture is vertically integrated, operating as seller, owner, operator, supplier, and contractor, so cycle time, quality control, and code compliance can be managed as one coordinated system rather than fragmented across multiple parties.

### 16.2 Replacement Strategy

In parallel with vacant-land delivery, Spectra’s replacement strategy is to raise and deploy revitalization capital in the same areas where new homes are delivered, so reinvestment is not isolated to new construction alone.

Where existing structures are salvageable and owners desire modernization, Spectra may contract directly with property owners to remodel and rehabilitate homes to improve safety, durability, and neighborhood appearance.

Where conditions warrant and lawful processes and community considerations support it, Spectra may also pursue demolition and replacement, substituting obsolete or unsafe structures with a Spectra home that meets modern resilience, efficiency, and code standards.

### 16.3 Master Developer Objective

Spectra’s overarching objective is to become a long-term municipal partner and, where appropriate, a Master Developer capable of coordinating targeted revitalization districts in alignment with the City’s Comprehensive Development Plan.

This includes a commitment to consider and implement the City’s adopted plans and to assist in making San Antonio a more self-sustaining community through housing delivery, infrastructure coordination, and neighborhood reinvestment.

Revitalization is not treated as a demolition-first posture. Spectra’s approach includes preserving historic buildings where feasible and appropriate, integrating adaptive reuse and context-sensitive design so redevelopment strengthens cultural assets rather than erasing them.

### 16.4 Safe Community Method

A core method for building safe, stable communities is designing for visibility, accountability, and resident stability.

Spectra’s approach emphasizes:

- ample street lighting;
- integrated camera systems on streets and around homes, subject to lawful standards and community protocols;
- neighborhood planning that supports predictable, family-centered living environments.

In placement and leasing / sales strategy, Spectra intends to reduce destabilizing mismatch by clustering similar household types where appropriate, such as families near families, designated senior-specific communities, and smaller households in configurations that support quieter living patterns.

## 17. Eastside Promise Neighborhood and Eastside Promise Zone

### 17.1 Eastside Promise Alignment and Place-Based Commitment

Spectra’s strategy explicitly aligns with the Eastside Promise Neighborhood and Eastside Promise Zone framework as a place-based commitment to strengthen existing neighborhoods, stabilize families, and guide investment into the East Side in a manner compatible with community character and long-term resident outcomes.

This is not positioned as speculative growth. It is positioned as a coordinated neighborhood stabilization and reinvestment initiative that supports the City’s intent to provide a revitalization toolbox to existing neighborhoods and developers.

### 17.2 Our Market — Families Already in the System

Spectra’s primary market for East Side delivery is families already connected to local organizations and agencies that serve households experiencing housing instability, overcrowding, cost burden, or prolonged waiting list conditions.

The strategy is designed to reduce the current dynamic where vulnerable families must compete in the open market against higher-income demand for the same limited inventory.

Spectra’s commitment is to work directly with qualified organizations, agencies, and referral partners to identify households with the longest documented waiting periods and prioritize those families for attainable housing opportunities as inventory is delivered.

### 17.3 Waiting List Prioritization and Fair, Documented Routing

To ensure transparency and fairness, Spectra will rely on documented eligibility and referral pathways through established organizations and agencies, with prioritization criteria that emphasize duration on waiting lists and verified housing need.

This supports the City’s broader objectives around reducing displacement pressure, improving household stability, and ensuring that new supply meaningfully serves residents who have been stuck in the system the longest.

### 17.4 Context-Sensitive Infill and Compatibility With Existing Neighborhoods

Spectra recognizes the City’s concern that recent infill development can raise compatibility issues related to neighborhood character, design, and perceived density.

East Side delivery will be governed by context-sensitive development standards that enhance neighborhood character rather than overwhelm it. This includes appropriate transitions, design discipline, and public realm improvements that reinforce safety, walkability, and long-term neighborhood pride.

### 17.5 Senior Stability and Housing Options as a Neighborhood Preservation Tool

East Side stabilization must include seniors who are already rooted in the neighborhood.

Spectra supports the City’s emphasis on enabling seniors to remain in their homes through programs that facilitate retrofitting, repairing, and maintaining existing housing stock, and also supports the creation of housing options for seniors who choose to relocate closer to walkable amenities and services.

### 17.6 Coordination With the City’s Interagency Collaborative and Corridor Revitalization Efforts

The City’s interagency collaborative approach, exemplified by REnewSA and related place-based coordination, reflects the operational reality that housing delivery, corridor revitalization, and infrastructure renewal must be synchronized across departments and partners.

Spectra’s East Side approach is structured to coordinate with City planning and community development leadership and align with the functions that influence successful delivery, including planning, historic preservation, development services, transportation and capital improvements, and housing partners.

### 17.7 Existing Site Conditions and Infrastructure Analysis

Spectra’s East Side redevelopment posture is strengthened by preliminary indicators that portions of the corridor contain the bones of prior residential activity and public infrastructure, such as existing roadway segments, curb and sidewalk conditions, drainage structures, utility corridor indicators, and fire protection features.

These conditions support an infrastructure-led redevelopment strategy focused on restoring and modernizing existing systems rather than assuming a raw-land development model.

The practical implication is that feasibility must be driven by verified infrastructure condition, capacity, and rehabilitation requirements because infrastructure readiness, not speculative unit targets, determines what can be delivered responsibly and compliantly.

### 17.8 Mixed-Use Intent — Residential and Commercial Reinvestment for Corridor Activation

Spectra’s East Side commitment is not limited to residential delivery. The intent includes coordinated commercial reinvestment where appropriate to support corridor activation, neighborhood-serving services, and local economic opportunity.

This mixed-use posture is consistent with the City’s recognition that vacant and underutilized parcels can be repositioned through a new mixture of uses that introduces housing, supports revitalization, and improves surrounding neighborhood conditions.

### 17.9 Vacant and Underutilized Parcels

The City’s planning framework recognizes that many inner-core areas contain vacant and underutilized parcels where existing buildings and zoning designations no longer meet market demand.

Spectra’s approach supports the City’s direction to allow redevelopment with a new mixture of uses, specifically including housing, to revitalize these areas and strengthen adjacent neighborhoods.
