## 1. ECONOMIC OVERVIEW

Whatcom County's economy is mid-size, diversified, and increasingly under pressure from housing-driven cost escalation.

| Metric | Value |
|--------|-------|
| Median Household Income (2024) | **$85,471** (FRED); $81,784 (QuickFacts) |
| Unemployment | ~4–5% (WA state average) |
| Poverty Rate | ~11–12% |
| Cost of Living vs. National Avg | **+20.8%** (WWU CBE, Q1 2025) |
| Foreign-Born Residents | 9.93% |

**Growth signal:** MHI jumped +$9,551 (+12.6%) from 2023→2024 per FRED, likely reflecting ACS rebenchmarking. Forward underwriting baseline should use $85,471.

**Key employers:** Western Washington University (17,000 students, ~3,200 employees) anchors the service economy. Healthcare (St. Joseph Hospital, PeaceHealth), maritime/fishing (Port of Bellingham Fairhaven Marine Industrial Park), and remote-work professionals round out the base. Tourism and Canadian cross-border spending provide secondary demand stimulus.

**Earning vs. living gap:** The county's +20.8% cost-of-living premium over national average means the $85K median income buys materially less than the same income in most peer metros. Workers earning $85K effectively have purchasing power equivalent to ~$70K nationally — yet housing prices are 40–60% above national median.

**Inventory data (May 2026):** Homes under $750K have <2 months inventory (firmly seller's market). Homes above $750K have 5+ months inventory (buyer's market emerging). The market is bifurcating around the $750K threshold.

**The income-to-housing chasm:**

At standard underwriting (30% gross DTI), a household earning the median $85,471 can support approximately **$385,000–$432,000** in mortgage financing. The median home now sells for $585,800–$750,000 — a structural gap of **$153,000 to $365,000** that conventional financing cannot bridge.

A household must earn **167% of Area Median Income ($180,591/year)** to afford a $750,000 median-priced home with a standard 3% down payment.

## 5. WORKFORCE SHORTAGES

Whatcom County faces a dual workforce crisis: workers cannot afford to live where the jobs are, and the housing that does exist is increasingly captured by non-resident owners and seasonal use.

**Manifestations:**

- **Teacher shortage:** Bellingham School District competes with remote-work salaries for a housing stock that teachers cannot afford — 972 homeless students signals the downstream effect on the workforce that serves children
- **Healthcare worker retention:** PeaceHealth and St. Joseph Hospital compete with tech-remote-worker purchasing power for the same housing stock
- **Trades and municipal workers:** The 59% professional employment share in the corridor (Semiahmoo) includes the workers who serve the community — nurses, teachers, municipal employees, first responders — who earn too much for subsidized housing and too little for market-rate ownership
- **Construction workforce:** Developer capacity is explicitly cited as a constraint — there are no large-scale affordable housing developers active in the corridor sub-market

**Self-reported homelessness cause (2024 PIT):**

| Cause | % Citing |
|-------|----------|
| Housing affordability | **47%** |
| Job loss / unemployment | 37% |
| Mental illness | 28% |
| Family rejection / kicked out | 24% |
| Physical health / disability | 22% |

## 6. INFRASTRUCTURE CONSTRAINTS

**Geographic constraints are the binding variable:**

- **Mountains, water, tribal land, and agricultural preservation** create a hard perimeter around buildable land in the Bellingham metro
- **Floodplain and climate constraints** limit development in the corridor (Birch Bay and Blaine have waterfront exposure and FEMA flood zones that complicate entitlement)
- **Water and sewer capacity** in the corridor is a known bottleneck — the county's September 2025 $6.6M investment was explicitly for infrastructure supporting the Telegraph Townhomes and LaFreniere Court pipeline
- **Transit** is limited outside the Bellingham metro, making the corridor heavily automobile-dependent — a structural disadvantage for workforce rental development

**The corridor developer gap:** There are **zero active affordable housing developers** in the Semiahmoo/Birch Bay/Blaine corridor. Kulshan CLT's pipeline is in the Bellingham metro. Any corridor deployment requires either recruiting a developer to the area or extending Kulshan CLT's geographic scope.

**HB 1110 (June 2026 deadline):** Washington State's Missing Middle mandate will force upzoning, but it does not address infrastructure capacity. The binding constraint may shift from zoning to utilities.

**Key risk:** The June 2026 HB 1110 compliance deadline creates both urgency and political turbulence — developers moving fast may face permit approval backlogs, and the state-mandated uniformity may compress development margins before first-movers can capitalize.

## 9. MANUFACTURING OPPORTUNITIES

**Pre-engineered / modular construction is specifically relevant here:**

The missing middle problem — 2-to-20 unit projects — is economically challenging for conventional stick-built construction. The per-unit cost, developer margin, and timeline make these projects marginal or infeasible.

**Spectra Holdings' vertically integrated ICF (insulated concrete form) construction model** addresses this gap:

## WHY THIS COUNTY MATTERS NOW

**Three overlapping windows are open simultaneously — and they close June 2026:**

1. **The state mandate window:** HB 1110 requires Bellingham to allow missing-middle housing by June 2026 or the state overrides. Developers who hold permits before that date can shape neighborhood character and economics before uniformity compresses margins.

2. **The political will window:** County Council has voted 7-0 on density, committed $6.6M to the pipeline, and adopted formal housing allocation resolutions. This level of bipartisan political commitment is rare and time-limited.

3. **The market window:** Homes under $750K remain below 2 months inventory — seller's market. The bifurcation above $750K suggests the market is beginning to correct at the top. The next 12 months may be the last optimal entry point before prices stabilize at a new, higher equilibrium.

**The crisis is local:** 69% of homeless individuals lost housing *in* Whatcom County. Every dollar of intervention stays local. This is not a border crisis or a migration problem — it is a community that cannot house its own workforce.
