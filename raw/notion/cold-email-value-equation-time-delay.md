# 04 - Variable 3: Time Delay
## How Hormozi frames it
<callout icon="ℹ️">
	*"Two agencies side by side, both saying they'll get your phone to ring. Agency A says: you're going to start recording ads, we're meeting three times a week an hour each time, you're going to spend four hours a week, and it's going to take us 60 days to get this stuff done. At day 60, your phone starts ringing. Agency B: you sign the paper, your credit card processes, and your phone rings within seconds. Same product. Time collapse."*
</callout>
\>
<callout icon="ℹ️">
	- Alex Hormozi
</callout>
Time delay is how long it takes the prospect to actually see the result.
Even if everything else in the email is compelling, one objection is almost always sitting in their head: *how long is this actually going to take?* Nobody wants to sign up for a six-month process with a stranger who sent them an unsolicited email. If you can't collapse the time in their head, the rest of the equation doesn't close.
---
## The common mistake
Spending half the email explaining the service.
<callout icon="ℹ️">
	*"Our team will first run discovery, then build your ICP, then launch test campaigns across three channels, then iterate over 90 days…"*
</callout>
Every word you spend explaining process is a word that pushes the result further into the future in the prospect's mind. What was a dream outcome two sentences ago has quietly turned into a project plan. That's a reply-rate killer.
The other common trap is the old-school 90-day promise:
<callout icon="ℹ️">
	*"If we could help you book 10-20 more calls in the next 90 days…"*
</callout>
90 days, 60 days, even 30 days - they all register as *too long*. The moment the timeline sounds like a full quarter, it stops feeling immediate, and you lose the emotional pull of the offer.
---
## The 2026 way
Surface **one early result** in the near future. The smaller the window, the better.
The best windows are **7 days or less**. Anything the prospect can receive from you *before* they commit to paid work is stronger still.
- *"Your first video launched in 7 days, no cost."*
- *"A full audit back in your inbox within 48 hours."*
- *"A tailored teardown video, sent Friday."*
Short windows do two things at once. They compress time delay, and they bump perceived likelihood - because a prospect can far more easily believe you'll deliver something small and fast than something large and slow.
Strip out everything that describes the multi-month process. Let one fast, concrete win do the work.
---

## The three clocks every prospect is running
Every cold email lands on a prospect who is already running three mental clocks. The best cold emails collapse all three:
1. **Clock 1 - time to reply.** Make the CTA low-friction enough that they can say yes today.
2. **Clock 2 - time to first value.** Get something concrete into their hands within a week, ideally before kickoff.
3. **Clock 3 - time to dream outcome.** Reframe the long-term timeline from *"in 90 days"* to *"the first measurable win by day X."*
If you can only collapse one of these clocks, collapse **Clock 2**. The first in-hand deliverable does the most work for reply rate, because it's the one the prospect can actually feel before they commit to anything.
---
## Why 7 beats 30, 60, and 90
There's a psychological shift when a number sinks below one week. Anything in the 30-90 day range gets mentally filed as *a project*. It requires planning, buy-in, budget cycles, and internal conversation. Anything at 7 days or less gets mentally filed as *a favor* - small enough to just say yes to.
Cold email lives and dies on the size of the commitment you're asking for. Keep your timeline in the "favor" range and your reply rates move.
---
## Checklist before sending
- [ ] Does the email promise a specific deliverable within a specific short window (ideally ≤7 days)?
- [ ] Have you deleted every multi-month timeline and every process description?
- [ ] Is the time window mentioned *before* any explanation of how you work?
- [ ] Does the first deliverable arrive *before* the prospect commits to paid work?
