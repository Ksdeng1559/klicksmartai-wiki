## Purpose
The **Mayor Economy Local Growth Operating Model** is a reusable framework for municipalities, regional development agencies, business associations, Indigenous economic-development organizations, investors, educational institutions, and entrepreneurs seeking to build a more productive and resilient local economy.
The model adapts the central lesson of China’s “mayor economy” to democratic and market-based local economies: local leadership should operate not only as an administrator and regulator, but also as a **market builder, ecosystem coordinator, investment facilitator, and long-term economic strategist**.
The municipality does not replace private enterprise. It creates the shared conditions that allow competitive businesses to start, expand, employ local people, attract capital, enter new markets, and reinvest locally.
> **Core proposition:** Local government sets a credible direction, builds shared capacity, coordinates institutions, and enables entrepreneurs and investors to create sustainable wealth.
## Executive Summary
Many local economies possess valuable assets—skilled people, land, infrastructure, institutions, established companies, cultural strengths, natural resources, and access to markets—but lack a coordinated system for converting those assets into durable prosperity.
The Mayor Economy model addresses this coordination gap. It organizes local economic development around a limited number of evidence-based industry clusters and connects municipal policy, infrastructure, talent, capital, entrepreneurship, procurement, and investment attraction into one operating system.
Under this approach, local leaders establish strategic priorities and remove structural barriers. Entrepreneurs identify market opportunities, create products and services, invest capital, hire workers, and build supply chains. Educational institutions develop the required talent. Investors and lenders finance viable projects. Community organizations ensure that growth produces broad local benefits.
Success is measured not by announcements, construction activity, or public spending alone, but by improvements in productivity, business formation and survival, median incomes, private investment, exports, quality employment, housing affordability, economic inclusion, and the resilience of the local tax base.
## The Local Growth Thesis
A local economy grows when it successfully combines:
**Local advantages + demonstrated market demand + entrepreneurial capacity + coordinated institutions + appropriate capital + enabling infrastructure.**
The model is based on five principles:
1. **Specialize where the region can win.** Select a small number of sectors supported by existing capabilities, market demand, and realistic competitive advantages.
2. **Build clusters rather than isolated projects.** Strengthen the complete network of producers, suppliers, service firms, workers, educators, financiers, infrastructure providers, and customers.
3. **Enable markets rather than substitute for them.** Public action should reduce barriers, supply public goods, and coordinate participants while preserving competition and private-sector discipline.
4. **Grow local firms while attracting complementary investment.** Existing businesses and entrepreneurs are primary engines of durable growth; outside investment should fill capability and supply-chain gaps.
5. **Measure community prosperity.** Economic development should increase productive capacity and translate into better incomes, opportunities, affordability, and public services.
## Strategic Outcomes
Each participating local economy should define measurable outcomes across five dimensions:
### Productive Economy
- Higher output and productivity per worker
- Increased business investment and modernization
- More firms selling outside the local market
- Stronger local supply chains and industry clusters
### Entrepreneurial Economy
- More viable business starts
- Higher three- and five-year business survival rates
- More local firms progressing from start-up to scale-up
- Improved access to customers, talent, facilities, expertise, and capital
### Inclusive Economy
- Growth in median household and employment income
- Greater participation by small businesses, Indigenous entrepreneurs, newcomers, youth, and underrepresented founders
- More accessible pathways into skilled employment and business ownership
- Increased local purchasing and community wealth retention
### Investable Economy
- A reliable pipeline of finance-ready projects and businesses
- Faster, clearer approval and site-development processes
- Increased private investment relative to public incentives
- Reduced uncertainty for entrepreneurs, lenders, and investors
### Resilient Economy
- Reduced dependence on a single employer or sector
- Improved housing, infrastructure, workforce, and climate resilience
- Stronger municipal revenues without excessive land-sale or debt dependence
- Greater capacity to adapt to technology, trade, demographic, and market changes
## Local Growth Operating System
The model can be implemented through eight connected functions.
### 1. Economic Intelligence
Create a continuously updated picture of the local economy, including:
- major employers and emerging firms;
- industry concentrations and supply chains;
- workforce capabilities and shortages;
- local purchasing and import leakage;
- available commercial and industrial sites;
- infrastructure constraints;
- business-expansion barriers;
- technology and market trends;
- capital gaps and investment opportunities.
**Primary output:** Local Economic Opportunity Map.
### 2. Cluster and Opportunity Selection
Select approximately three to five priority clusters or opportunity themes using transparent criteria:
- evidence of customer demand;
- existing local capabilities;
- potential for productivity and wage growth;
- ability to sell beyond the region;
- supply-chain depth;
- private investment interest;
- environmental and community fit;
- realistic differentiation from competing regions.
**Primary output:** Priority Cluster Portfolio with rationale, targets, and accountable leaders.
### 3. Business Retention and Scale-Up
Treat existing firms as a core economic-development portfolio. Assign relationship managers or sector leads to identify:
- expansion plans;
- financing requirements;
- succession and acquisition opportunities;
- workforce needs;
- permitting or infrastructure barriers;
- export potential;
- technology-adoption opportunities;
- supplier and customer introductions.
**Primary output:** Local Business Growth Pipeline.
### 4. Entrepreneur Participation Platform
Create a defined pathway through which entrepreneurs can participate in the local growth strategy.
Entrepreneurs should be able to:
- view priority economic opportunities and unmet local needs;
- submit business or project concepts;
- join industry working groups;
- access procurement opportunities;
- request site, permit, training, financing, or market support;
- connect with anchor institutions, customers, suppliers, and investors;
- participate in pilot projects and innovation challenges;
- receive guidance on becoming investment- or lender-ready.
**Primary output:** Entrepreneur Opportunity Portal and Opportunity Registry.
### 5. Talent and Workforce Alignment
Connect employers, schools, colleges, training providers, labour organizations, and community agencies around verified occupational demand.
Programs should support:
- employer-designed curricula;
- apprenticeships and work-integrated learning;
- rapid retraining for priority occupations;
- entrepreneurship and management education;
- newcomer and youth pathways;
- recruitment and retention of specialized talent.
**Primary output:** Cluster Workforce Plan.
### 6. Infrastructure and Regulatory Enablement
Prioritize public investments and process improvements that reduce business costs and unlock private investment, including:
- serviced employment land;
- transportation and logistics;
- utilities and broadband;
- workforce and attainable housing;
- digital approvals and coordinated permitting;
- shared production, laboratory, commercial kitchen, or innovation facilities;
- clear development timelines and accountable service standards.
**Primary output:** Economic Infrastructure and Barrier-Removal Plan.
### 7. Capital and Investment Coordination
Build a local capital network involving banks, credit unions, community lenders, private investors, government programs, foundations, development funds, and major institutions.
The network should help match viable opportunities with:
- start-up and working capital;
- equipment and expansion financing;
- real-estate and infrastructure capital;
- equity and patient capital;
- grants and repayable public funding;
- succession and acquisition finance.
Public incentives should be conditional, transparent, time-limited, and tied to measurable outcomes.
**Primary output:** Investable Project and Business Pipeline.
### 8. Market Access and Procurement
Use local anchor institutions—including municipalities, health systems, educational institutions, utilities, housing providers, and major employers—to expand market access for capable local firms.
Subject to procurement law and competitive value, the region can support:
- supplier-readiness programs;
- transparent opportunity forecasts;
- smaller contract lots accessible to SMEs;
- innovation pilots;
- apprenticeship and community-benefit requirements;
- introductions to national and export markets.
**Primary output:** Local Supplier and Market Access Program.
## Entrepreneur Participation Framework
Entrepreneurs are not secondary beneficiaries of the model; they are its principal wealth-creation partners.
### Ways Entrepreneurs Can Participate
1. **Start a business that fills a cluster gap.** Use the Opportunity Map to identify missing suppliers, services, technologies, and customer solutions.
2. **Expand an existing business.** Align expansion plans with priority sectors, workforce programs, infrastructure investments, and financing channels.
3. **Become a local supplier.** Qualify for municipal, institutional, developer, and anchor-employer procurement opportunities.
4. **Commercialize a local solution.** Develop products addressing housing, transportation, energy, health, food, tourism, manufacturing, or public-service challenges.
5. **Acquire or succeed an existing business.** Preserve local employment and ownership through business succession and acquisition.
6. **Form a consortium or cooperative.** Combine capabilities with other SMEs to bid on larger contracts or enter new markets.
7. **Invest in enabling assets.** Develop facilities, workforce housing, logistics, shared production capacity, or technology platforms needed by the cluster.
8. **Mentor and reinvest.** Experienced entrepreneurs can support new founders, angel networks, accelerators, and local investment funds.
### Entrepreneur Entry Process
Each local economy can adopt the following standard pathway:
**Discover → Assess → Connect → Validate → Finance → Pilot → Scale → Reinvest**
- **Discover:** Review published opportunities, cluster priorities, and procurement forecasts.
- **Assess:** Complete a short business-capability and readiness assessment.
- **Connect:** Meet the appropriate sector lead, customer, supplier, trainer, or capital partner.
- **Validate:** Confirm customer demand, economics, regulatory requirements, and competitive fit.
- **Finance:** Build an appropriate funding package based on project stage and risk.
- **Pilot:** Test the solution with a customer or anchor institution.
- **Scale:** Expand production, hiring, sales, and market reach.
- **Reinvest:** Purchase locally, train workers, mentor founders, or invest in the next opportunity.
## Governance Model
A credible Mayor Economy strategy requires shared governance rather than dependence on one political leader.
### Local Growth Council
Suggested membership:
- mayor or regional chair;
- municipal chief administrative and economic-development leadership;
- entrepreneurs and SME representatives;
- major employers and anchor institutions;
- Indigenous economic-development representatives;
- education and workforce partners;
- lenders and investors;
- labour and community organizations;
- infrastructure and utility partners.
### Responsibilities
- approve strategic clusters and measurable outcomes;
- maintain a transparent opportunity pipeline;
- coordinate cross-institutional barriers and investments;
- review performance quarterly;
- protect competitive neutrality and public value;
- publish results, lessons, and corrective actions.
Each priority cluster should have a private-sector co-chair, a public-sector lead, an annual action plan, and defined success measures.
## Decision Rules and Safeguards
Before committing public resources, decision-makers should confirm:
- Is there demonstrated customer demand?
- Will credible private participants invest capital or assume risk?
- Does the initiative strengthen an existing or attainable local advantage?
- Will it create durable productivity, income, or employment gains?
- Can it survive without permanent subsidy?
- Does it complement rather than unfairly displace competitive firms?
- Are benefits, costs, risks, and selection criteria transparent?
- What is the cost per job, business, dollar of private investment, or unit of public value created?
- What happens if the project underperforms?
Avoid strategies based primarily on prestige projects, speculative real estate, politically selected firms, permanent subsidies, excessive municipal debt, or announcements unsupported by customer demand.
## Core Performance Scorecard
Each region should establish a baseline and publish quarterly or annual performance against a small number of measures.
### Business and Investment
- Net new employer businesses
- Three- and five-year survival rate
- Number and value of business expansions
- Private capital invested
- Ratio of private investment to public support
- Number of finance-ready opportunities
### Employment and Income
- Net quality jobs created or retained
- Median employment income
- Productivity per worker
- Apprenticeships and work-integrated placements
- Priority occupations filled
### Cluster and Market Growth
- Revenue generated outside the region
- Number of exporting or interregional firms
- Local supplier spending
- Supply-chain gaps filled
- New products, patents, or commercial pilots
### Community Outcomes
- Housing affordability and workforce-housing availability
- Participation of underrepresented entrepreneurs
- Local ownership retained through succession
- Municipal tax-base diversification
- Resident satisfaction and economic mobility
## 12-Month Adoption Roadmap
### Phase 1 — Diagnose: Months 1–3
- Establish the Local Growth Council.
- Complete the economic asset, supply-chain, workforce, capital, and barrier assessment.
- Interview local firms and entrepreneurs.
- Define the baseline scorecard.
- Identify an initial portfolio of opportunities.
### Phase 2 — Prioritize: Months 3–5
- Select three to five priority clusters or themes.
- Appoint public and private cluster leads.
- Publish the Local Economic Opportunity Map.
- Identify quick wins and enabling infrastructure requirements.
### Phase 3 — Mobilize: Months 5–8
- Launch the Entrepreneur Opportunity Portal.
- Establish the business scale-up and capital pipelines.
- Align workforce and procurement programs.
- Resolve a first set of high-impact regulatory or infrastructure barriers.
### Phase 4 — Pilot: Months 8–10
- Launch two to five market-validated pilot initiatives.
- Secure participating customers, entrepreneurs, investors, and delivery partners.
- Define performance milestones and exit conditions for public support.
### Phase 5 — Scale and Report: Months 10–12
- Evaluate pilot results.
- Scale successful initiatives and discontinue weak ones.
- Publish the first Local Growth Performance Report.
- Refresh priorities and the opportunity portfolio for the following year.

## Definition of Success
The model succeeds when the local economy develops a repeatable capability to identify opportunities, organize stakeholders, prepare entrepreneurs, mobilize capital, validate demand, execute projects, and scale successful businesses—without depending permanently on subsidies or a single political champion.
The desired long-term cycle is:
> **Local intelligence → opportunity identification → entrepreneurial response → coordinated support → private investment → market validation → business growth → quality employment → local reinvestment.**
This turns economic development from a collection of disconnected programs into a measurable **Local Growth Operating System**.
## Sun Tzu Strategic Interpretation
The Mayor Economy can also be interpreted through the strategic logic associated with **Sun Tzu’s \*The Art of War**\*. This should be treated as a modern analytical lens rather than a claim that the Mayor Economy was formally derived from Sun Tzu.
Under this interpretation, the mayor or municipal Party secretary acts as the chief economic strategist of the region. Their role is not to operate every business, but to understand the economic terrain, identify favourable positions, coordinate resources, build alliances, reduce friction, and create conditions in which local enterprises can succeed.
> **Strategic proposition:** Government shapes the terrain, entrepreneurs compete on it, investors finance it, institutions supply talent and knowledge, and customers determine whether the strategy succeeds.
### 1. Know the Local Economy and the Competitive Environment
A successful local strategy begins with economic intelligence.
Each region should understand:
- its strongest industries, skills, institutions, infrastructure, and natural assets;
- its weaknesses, bottlenecks, capital gaps, and workforce shortages;
- competing regions and their advantages;
- changing customer demand, technology, demographics, trade patterns, and public policy;
- supply-chain gaps and underserved markets.
The practical lesson is that local economic development should begin with evidence and intelligence, not prestige projects or assumptions.
### 2. Use Economic Terrain to Create Advantage
In a local economy, “terrain” includes more than geography. It includes:
- transportation and logistics;
- land, utilities, broadband, and energy;
- permitting and regulation;
- workforce availability;
- housing affordability;
- access to capital;
- supplier and customer networks;
- institutional relationships.
The municipality’s role is to improve this terrain by lowering transaction costs, accelerating approvals, improving infrastructure, aligning training, and connecting firms to markets and capital.
### 3. Create Advantage Before Competing
A region should build advantages before entering direct competition for investment.
Durable advantages may include:
- specialized workforce pipelines;
- established supplier networks;
- industry-specific infrastructure;
- strong university and research partnerships;
- faster and more predictable approvals;
- anchor-customer relationships;
- local procurement and pilot opportunities;
- coordinated financing pathways.
The objective is to make the region the logical choice before a company, entrepreneur, or investor makes a final decision.
### 4. Avoid Competing Where Others Are Strongest
Local economies should not attempt to copy larger or better-capitalized regions directly.
Instead, they should identify narrow positions where local capabilities intersect with unmet demand. Examples might include:
- modular housing and construction technology;
- agricultural automation;
- clean-energy components;
- specialized logistics;
- mortgage and property technology;
- Indigenous infrastructure services;
- regional health or tourism services.
The strategic principle is:
> **Do not compete on the strongest point of another region. Build around a gap where the local economy can establish a defensible advantage.**
### 5. Concentrate Resources
A Sun Tzu-informed local strategy avoids spreading resources across too many disconnected initiatives.
Municipalities should select a limited number of priority clusters and align:
- infrastructure;
- land use;
- workforce training;
- procurement;
- capital programs;
- investment attraction;
- marketing;
- business support.
Concentrated and coordinated resources create stronger cluster effects than numerous isolated programs.
### 6. Use Intelligence Before Capital
Public spending should follow verified opportunity, not substitute for it.
Before committing capital, local leaders should ask:
- What confirmed customer demand exists?
- Which firms are preparing to expand?
- What products and services are imported because no local supplier exists?
- What prevents local entrepreneurs from scaling?
- Which capability gaps weaken the cluster?
- What evidence shows that private participants will invest or purchase?
The strongest Mayor Economy model is therefore supported by a permanent **Local Economic Intelligence System**.
### 7. Build Strategic Alliances
Local prosperity requires coordinated alliances among:
- entrepreneurs and SMEs;
- major employers and anchor institutions;
- banks, credit unions, and investors;
- universities, colleges, and training providers;
- Indigenous nations and economic-development organizations;
- neighbouring municipalities;
- provincial, state, and federal governments;
- utilities, infrastructure providers, and industry associations.
The mayor or regional leader becomes an alliance architect who brings together capabilities that no single institution controls.
### 8. Create Momentum and Leverage
Economic clusters create cumulative momentum:
> **Anchor demand → entrepreneurs and suppliers → skilled workers → training → investment → new firms → exports → reinvestment.**
The role of public leadership is to initiate, coordinate, and accelerate this cycle until the cluster becomes increasingly self-sustaining.
### 9. Adapt Through Experimentation
A local strategy should be treated as a portfolio of market-tested experiments rather than a rigid long-term plan.
The region should:
1. identify an opportunity;
2. validate customer demand;
3. launch a limited pilot;
4. measure commercial and community outcomes;
5. scale successful models;
6. revise or discontinue weak initiatives.
This approach preserves strategic direction while allowing adaptation to changing conditions.
## Sun Tzu–Mayor Economy Operating Formula
The combined model can be expressed as:
**Local prosperity = intelligence × positioning × coordination × execution**
- **Intelligence:** Understand the market, local capabilities, competitors, risks, and opportunities.
- **Positioning:** Select sectors and opportunities where the region has a credible advantage.
- **Coordination:** Align government, entrepreneurs, capital, talent, infrastructure, and institutions.
- **Execution:** Move opportunities through validation, approvals, financing, production, hiring, market entry, and scale.
Because the factors multiply rather than simply add, a major weakness in any one area can undermine the complete strategy.
## Entrepreneur Role Within the Strategy
Entrepreneurs are the commercial operators who convert regional strategy into market-tested activity.
They participate by:
- identifying unmet demand;
- building businesses that fill cluster gaps;
- organizing suppliers and partnerships;
- developing products and services;
- raising and deploying capital;
- accepting commercial risk;
- hiring and training workers;
- entering outside markets;
- reinvesting locally.
The municipality may identify a strategic opportunity, but entrepreneurs must validate it through customers, revenue, and operational performance.
## Ethical and Governance Guardrails
The Sun Tzu interpretation should not be used to justify secrecy, manipulation, favouritism, or destructive competition between communities.
Its useful applications are:
- disciplined preparation;
- intelligence gathering;
- strategic positioning;
- efficient use of resources;
- alliance building;
- adaptability;
- avoidance of wasteful confrontation.
All public participation should remain transparent, competitively neutral, legally compliant, and tied to measurable public value.
## Integrated Strategic Summary
The Mayor Economy can be understood as **municipal economic statecraft**. Sun Tzu contributes the strategic logic: understand the terrain, gather intelligence, concentrate resources, build alliances, create favourable conditions, and execute through coordinated actors.
Combined with the Local Growth Operating System, the model becomes:
> **Understand the terrain → choose a defensible position → coordinate the ecosystem → enable entrepreneurs → validate through markets → scale what works → reinvest locally.**
## Evolution of the Mayor Economy
The Mayor Economy should be understood as an evolving political-economic model rather than a fixed formula. Its development can be divided into four broad stages.
### Stage 1 — Decentralized Local Entrepreneurialism: 1980s–1990s
Following the launch of reform and opening-up in 1978, fiscal and administrative decentralization gave local governments greater control over revenues, land, industrial policy, and local development decisions. At the same time, promotion systems increasingly rewarded officials for GDP growth, fixed-asset investment, fiscal receipts, and visible development outcomes.
This created a competitive **promotion tournament** among provinces, municipalities, and counties. Mayors and municipal Party secretaries became de facto chief economic strategists who:
- negotiated directly with domestic and foreign investors;
- allocated industrial land;
- established development zones;
- expanded infrastructure;
- restructured local state-owned enterprises;
- coordinated banks, schools, utilities, and administrative agencies around growth targets.
The central achievement of this stage was the creation of strong local incentives to experiment, attract capital, and accelerate industrialization.
### Stage 2 — Land Finance and Infrastructure-Led Expansion: 1990s–2000s
The transferability of urban land-use rights enabled municipal governments to monetize land and use the proceeds to finance roads, transit, utilities, industrial parks, public services, and investor incentives. This system became known as **land finance** or *tǔdì cáizhèng*.
Local Government Financing Vehicles were subsequently used to borrow against expected future land and development revenues. This allowed cities to build infrastructure before sufficient tax revenues had been generated.
The model contributed to rapid urbanization and industrial expansion, but it also created structural dependence on:
- rising land values;
- property development;
- continuous investment growth;
- municipal borrowing;
- future land-sale revenue.
### Stage 3 — Strategic Industrial Investment and Local State Capitalism: 2000s–2010s
As regions became more sophisticated, some local governments moved beyond land development and began acting as strategic investors. Municipal authorities used development funds, public companies, industrial guidance funds, equity investments, subsidies, and procurement to build targeted industries.
Examples commonly associated with this stage include:
- **Shenzhen**, which developed into a technology and advanced-manufacturing centre through experimentation, foreign investment, infrastructure, and entrepreneurial ecosystems;
- **Suzhou**, whose industrial development included the China–Singapore Suzhou Industrial Park and strong integration into global manufacturing networks;
- **Hefei**, which used public investment and industrial strategy to support companies and sectors such as advanced displays, electric vehicles, and semiconductors.
At its best, this stage demonstrated how local government could coordinate capital, infrastructure, talent, and market access to create new industrial capabilities. At its worst, it encouraged politically selected firms, duplicated investment, overcapacity, and weak commercial discipline.
### Stage 4 — High-Quality Development and Recentralization: 2010s–Present
The classic Mayor Economy began to change as the central government increased fiscal oversight, anti-corruption enforcement, debt controls, environmental requirements, and national industrial coordination.
Performance expectations increasingly shifted away from GDP and construction volume alone toward **high-quality development**, including:
- innovation and technological capability;
- green transition and environmental performance;
- financial stability and debt reduction;
- productivity and industrial upgrading;
- regional integration;
- improved household welfare and common prosperity.
Regional strategies such as the Greater Bay Area and Yangtze River Delta also reduced the emphasis on isolated municipal competition and promoted more coordinated economic planning across city regions.
Under this emerging model, mayors have become less autonomous as independent economic entrepreneurs and more responsible for implementing national and regional priorities within tighter institutional controls.
### Achievements of the Classic Model
The Mayor Economy helped solve a major development problem: **institutional coordination**. It enabled cities to align land, infrastructure, finance, workforce development, industrial policy, and investment attraction around a shared growth agenda.
Its major contributions included:
- rapid industrialization and urbanization;
- large-scale infrastructure delivery;
- development of export-oriented manufacturing clusters;
- attraction of foreign and domestic investment;
- local policy experimentation;
- creation of regionally specialized industrial ecosystems.
### Structural Risks and Distortions
The same concentration of authority that supported rapid execution also generated systemic risks:
- dependence on land sales and real-estate appreciation;
- excessive Local Government Financing Vehicle debt;
- speculative construction and property bubbles;
- duplicated industrial capacity among competing cities;
- environmental degradation;
- corruption and political favouritism;
- preference for visible investment over household income, productivity, and long-term resilience.
These risks demonstrate that strong local economic leadership must be paired with transparent governance, market validation, debt discipline, competitive neutrality, and measurable public value.
### Evolution into the Local Growth Operating Model
The contemporary Local Growth Operating Model preserves the most valuable lesson of the Mayor Economy—local leadership can coordinate institutions and shape favourable economic conditions—while removing its most dangerous features.

> **Evolutionary proposition:** The classic Mayor Economy mobilized land, authority, and capital to accelerate growth. The evolved model mobilizes intelligence, institutions, entrepreneurs, and disciplined investment to produce sustainable community prosperity.
The objective is therefore not to reproduce China’s historical system. It is to adapt its coordination capability to a democratic, transparent, market-based framework in which government shapes the economic terrain, private enterprise creates wealth, and measurable community outcomes determine success.
