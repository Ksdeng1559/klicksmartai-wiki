5 · Advance-Fee Concern — Detailed Analysis

5.1 What makes an advance fee problematic in CRE financing

The U.S. Federal Trade Commission publishes a clear list — "Six Sure Signs of an Advance-Fee Loan Scam." The pattern this firm matches is:

FTC red flagApplies here?
(1) Lender not interested in your credit history — "Bad credit? No problem"Unconfirmed — needs verification of term sheet
(2) Fees not disclosed clearly / up-front fee for "processing" or "insurance"Yes — match
(3) Lender doesn't give you a written application / disclosureUnconfirmed — request the application package

(5) Lender not registered in your stateYes — not registered with NYDFS / NMLS

5.2 Why an up-front fee is not industry standard

Legitimate CRE lending and broker practice:

  • Borrower-paid loan fees (origination, processing, application) are typically deducted from the loan proceeds at closing or collected only after a firm approval. They are never required before a lender commitment exists.
  • Third-party fees (appraisal, environmental, title) are paid directly to the third party — not to the broker or "lender."
  • Broker placement fees are typically paid by the lender at closing (0.5–2 points), not by the borrower.
  • If a fee is collected and the deal does not close, a legitimate firm refunds it. A scam firm disappears.

5.3 FinCEN data on CRE advance-fee fraud

"Advance Fee Schemes: Filers described various forms of advance fee schemes targeting borrowers, lenders, and companies unable to obtain CRE financing. These schemes involved fraudulent business proposals and financial instruments. … A filer reported that an individual generated over a million dollars in an advance fee scheme by targeting CRE developers unable to secure financing during the economic downturn. … The highest amounts reported involved advance fee schemes. Two filers regulated by the OCC reported advance fee schemes for $780 million and another filer regulated by the Federal Reserve reported an advance fee scheme for $500 million." — FinCEN, Commercial Real Estate Financing Fraud advisory

6 · What To Do Now

  1. Stop payment. Do not pay any further amount. If a wire was sent today, contact your bank immediately and request a wire recall — same-day recall is the realistic recovery window.
  2. Document everything. Save every email, especially the message that described the fee, the wire instructions received, the name on the receiving account, any term sheet or commitment letter, and who initiated contact (cold call, referral, LinkedIn DM).
  3. Verify NY entity. Run a search at apps.dos.ny.gov/publicInquiry/ for "Beekman Wilshire." Confirm the LLC exists, who the registered agent is, when it was formed, and whether any fund entity is also registered.
  4. Test the refund. Ask them in writing: "If the loan does not close, is this fee refundable, and on what terms?" A legitimate firm will answer in writing with a clear refund policy. A scam firm will not.
  5. If you wired money:
    • Call your bank's fraud / wire recall team now.
    • File an IC3 complaint (ic3.gov) if criminal fraud is suspected.
    • File with the FTC (ReportFraud.ftc.gov) and CFPB (consumerfinance.gov/complaint).
    • File with the NY Attorney General (ag.ny.gov) and BBB Scam Tracker.
  6. If only verbal and no payment yet: simply stop responding. Block the email and phone number. Do not engage further.

Walk away from Beekman Wilshire regardless of whether the advance fee is recoverable. The firm has:
