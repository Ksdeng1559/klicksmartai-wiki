# The $432,000 Gap: What's Really Happening in Whatcom County's Housing Market

*An investigative look at Bellingham's affordability crisis — and the community-led solutions emerging from the crisis*

---

## The Number Nobody Is Talking About

There is a figure that doesn't appear in county planning documents or city council agendas. It's not hidden — it's just hiding in plain sight, buried in mortgage calculators and Zillow searches.

That number is **$432,000**.

Here's how we got there:

- A family of four earning Whatcom County's median household income — **$106,000/year** — can afford a home priced at approximately **$318,000**. That's assuming a standard 30-year fixed mortgage at today's rates, a 3% down payment, and the standard 28% front-end debt ratio.
- The median home selling price in Whatcom County today: **$750,000**.

The gap: $432,000.

That's not a market inefficiency. That's not a temporary imbalance. That's a structural wall — built into the geography, the regulation, and the economics of one of the most desirable small cities in the Pacific Northwest.

---

## But Bellingham Wasn't Always Like This

Five years ago, a median-income household could still find a path to homeownership in Whatcom County. The median home price hovered around $400,000 — stretchable with a good down payment and a rate buy-down, but reachable.

Then three things happened in sequence:

**First:** Remote work flattened the geography of housing demand. Knowledge workers from Seattle, the Bay Area, and Los Angeles discovered Bellingham — a university town with mountain access, a food scene, and prices that looked like a bargain compared to where they were coming from. They arrived with higher down payments, stronger credit, and sometimes all-cash offers.

**Second:** Interest rates compressed purchase affordability even as prices continued climbing. A $500,000 home at 3% mortgage meant a $2,100/month payment. The same home at 7% means $3,300/month. The price didn't have to move much for the monthly payment to become out of reach.

**Third:** The regulatory environment limited the supply response. Single-family zoning dominance, long permit timelines, and construction cost inflation made it economically impossible to build enough homes to meet demand.

The result: the home that a Bellingham teacher, a nurse, a small business owner, or a young family could have afforded in 2019 now requires an income that exceeds what 167% of Whatcom County households earn.

---

## The County Knows. The State Is Stepping In.

Whatcom County's own 5-Year Homeless Housing Plan — published in September 2025 — formally acknowledges a **5,000-unit shortfall** over five years. The plan doesn't dress it up: the county's housing program estimates that nearly 5,000 new affordable units are needed immediately, and funding for that construction remains substantially undersubscribed.

The State of Washington responded in 2023 with **HB 1110**, the Missing Middle Housing Bill, which mandates that Bellingham and other cities allow a broader range of housing types — ADUs, duplexes, triplexes, courtyard apartments — in all residential zones. The compliance deadline is **June 2026**. If Bellingham doesn't move fast enough, the state overrides local zoning.

Mayor Kim Lund signed an executive order in November 2024 to accelerate compliance. The City Council voted in April 2025 to increase density within half a mile of major transit stops, moving from 4 to 6 units per lot.

This is not a city that's asleep at the switch. It's a city that's trying to catch up to a problem that built up over a decade.

## Why the Braided Model Is the Right Answer

The county's own plan endorses something called the **braided funding model** — combining public grants, nonprofit land stewardship, and private investment in a single project structure. Each layer reduces the risk and cost of the others.

Community land trusts — most notably the **Kulshan Community Land Trust (Kulshan CLT)** — are the anchor. When a land trust owns the land, it can sell the home at below-market price while maintaining permanent affordability. The homeowner builds equity; the land trust preserves the community benefit.

Private capital fills the development gap. Foundation money and CDFIs (Community Development Financial Institutions) provide patient equity at below-market rates. County infrastructure grants reduce per-unit hard costs. When these pieces fit together, the math works — even at $750K median values.

This model is not theoretical. It's active in communities across Washington State. Enterprise Community Partners, the Opportunity Finance Network, and Washington CDFIs are actively seeking projects to finance. The pipeline exists. The question is whether the developer capacity and the public-private coordination exists locally to move fast enough.

## The Real Test: Faith, Community, and Patient Capital

What makes the Whatcom County moment different from other housing markets is the community alignment. The Interfaith Coalition of Whatcom County is actively engaged in housing advocacy. Local congregations — St. James Presbyterian, St. Paul's Episcopal, Bellingham Unitarian Fellowship — have programs reaching vulnerable residents.

This is not just a housing market. It's a community that understands the moral weight of the problem.

That's why the investment framing here matters. This is not about speculative returns. It's about patient capital — aligned with community values, structured for long-term hold, and designed to produce both financial returns and community outcomes.

The family earning $106K a year shouldn't need to earn $180K to live in the community they've helped build.

The solution exists. The question is who steps in to be part of it.
