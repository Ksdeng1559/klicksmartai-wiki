# Breakthrough Management — Urban Mining Field Validation Plan

**Project:** Breakthrough Circular Carbon Infrastructure Platform  
**Workstream:** Urban Mining / E-Waste / Critical Materials Recovery  
**Prepared by:** KlickSmartAI  
**Status:** Field Validation Draft  
**Purpose:** Convert Breakthrough Management’s direct field observations into a structured validation process for grant funding, pilot design, SBIR/STTR concept papers, and partner outreach.

---

## 1. Executive Summary

Breakthrough Management is evaluating Urban Mining as the primary technical and commercial beachhead for its circular carbon infrastructure strategy.

The current working thesis is:

> Existing recycling depots, e-waste processors, and material recovery operations remain heavily manual. This creates an opportunity for AI-assisted, human-in-the-loop urban mining workflows that improve classification, safety, throughput, chain-of-custody, recovery reporting, and critical materials recovery.

The immediate goal is not to build a fully automated plant. The stronger and more fundable opportunity is to validate a partially automated, AI-assisted recovery system that augments existing workers and improves current manual workflows.

---

## 2. Field Observation Starting Point

Gilbert, Managing Director of Breakthrough Management, observed first-hand that current recycling depots often do not use meaningful automation. Instead, facilities rely on manual sorting, manual removal, manual staging, and labor-intensive processing.

This field observation is important because it turns the opportunity from a speculative technology idea into a real operational improvement problem.

The validation process should now determine whether this observation is isolated or recurring across multiple urban mining and recycling environments.

---

## 3. Urban Mining Definition for This Workstream

For this project, Urban Mining means the recovery of valuable materials from discarded products, waste streams, and industrial residues.

Priority feedstocks include:

1. Data-center server decommissioning equipment
2. GPUs, CPUs, circuit boards, and high-value electronics
3. Consumer electronics
4. Industrial electronics
5. Metal shredder residue
6. Appliances and mixed equipment
7. Construction and demolition metals
8. Batteries and battery-containing devices, subject to safety controls

The target is not generic recycling. The target is higher-value material recovery with data-backed chain-of-custody and grant-ready reporting.

---

## 4. Validation Objectives

The field validation process should answer six core questions:

1. Are existing urban mining and e-waste workflows primarily manual?
2. Which manual tasks create the largest bottlenecks?
3. Which tasks create the greatest safety risks?
4. Which materials offer the highest recovery value?
5. Where is chain-of-custody and MRV reporting weakest?
6. Which AI-assisted or semi-automated intervention would create the fastest measurable improvement?

---

## 5. Core Hypotheses

### Hypothesis 1 — Manual Workflow Gap

Most recycling depots and e-waste recovery operations rely heavily on manual sorting, identification, component removal, and staging.

### Hypothesis 2 — Data and Chain-of-Custody Gap

Manual workflows often lack structured digital tracking, making it difficult to produce reliable recovery reports, environmental impact data, and grant-ready MRV evidence.

### Hypothesis 3 — Safety and Hazard Gap

Battery-containing devices, sharp materials, heavy components, and hazardous parts create safety risks that could be reduced through AI-assisted detection and operator guidance.

### Hypothesis 4 — Recovery Optimization Gap

Manual sorting may miss or under-prioritize higher-value materials such as PCBs, copper, aluminum, gold-bearing components, lithium batteries, rare earth-containing parts, and server-related hardware.

### Hypothesis 5 — Partial Automation Is More Fundable Than Full Automation

A human-in-the-loop system is more realistic, lower-risk, workforce-friendly, and fundable than a fully automated recycling plant.

## 8. Interview Questions

### Operator Questions

1. What materials do you process most frequently?
2. Which materials are most profitable?
3. Which materials are hardest to sort?
4. Which steps are still manual?
5. What slows down throughput?
6. What creates safety concerns?
7. How do you identify batteries or hazardous components?
8. How do you track material from intake to sale?
9. Do you currently use cameras, scanners, robotics, or AI?
10. What would make your recovery workflow faster or safer?

### Data and Reporting Questions

1. How is incoming material recorded?
2. Are weights recorded by category?
3. Are recovered materials tracked by type?
4. Can you produce chain-of-custody reports?
5. Can you produce environmental impact reports?
6. Can you estimate avoided landfill, recovered metals, or emissions impact?
7. What documentation do downstream buyers require?
8. What reports would help with grants, compliance, or customer reporting?

### Pilot Interest Questions

1. Would you consider testing an AI-assisted intake or sorting system?
2. Which workflow station would be easiest to test first?
3. What performance metrics would matter most?
4. Would you provide anonymized operational data for a pilot?
5. Would you support a grant application as a partner, pilot site, or letter-of-support provider?

## 9. Proposed First Pilot Scope

The recommended first pilot is:

> AI-assisted e-waste intake, classification, hazard detection, and chain-of-custody reporting.

This pilot avoids the complexity of full plant automation while proving a fundable technical wedge.

### Pilot Components

1. Camera-based material intake station
2. AI visual classification model
3. Battery and hazard detection support
4. Human operator review interface
5. Material category tagging
6. Weight and batch tracking
7. Chain-of-custody log
8. Recovery report dashboard
9. MRV-ready environmental impact output

---

## 10. Human-in-the-Loop Workflow

```text
Inbound e-waste / recyclable material
    ↓
AI-assisted intake and classification
    ↓
Computer vision material identification
    ↓
Hazard and battery detection support
    ↓
Human operator review
    ↓
Semi-automated sorting or disassembly assistance
    ↓
Quality control and exception handling
    ↓
Material recovery and staging
    ↓
AI-MRV chain-of-custody reporting
```

## 12. Success Metrics

The field validation process should define measurable pilot metrics.

| Metric | Example Measurement |
|---|---|
| Sorting time reduction | % reduction in operator sorting time |
| Classification accuracy | % accuracy by material class |
| Battery / hazard detection | % detected before manual handling |
| Throughput improvement | lbs/hour or units/hour |
| Recovery value improvement | $ recovered per ton or per batch |
| Chain-of-custody completeness | % of batches with complete digital records |
| Reporting speed | time to produce recovery/environmental report |
| Worker safety | reduction in risky manual handling steps |
| Grant evidence quality | number of metrics available for grant reporting |

## 14. Go / No-Go Criteria

### Proceed if:

- At least 3 facilities confirm heavily manual workflows
- At least 2 facilities identify safety or hazard-detection pain points
- At least 1 facility expresses pilot interest
- There is evidence of weak digital chain-of-custody
- At least one high-value material stream is available
- A fundable pilot can be scoped under $250K–$750K

### Pause or revise if:

- Facilities already have strong automation
- Operators do not see AI assistance as useful
- Feedstock access is weak
- Recovery economics are too low
- No partner is willing to provide data, access, or support
