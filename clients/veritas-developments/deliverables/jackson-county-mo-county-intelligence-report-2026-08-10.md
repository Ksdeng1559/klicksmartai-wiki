## 1. ECONOMIC OVERVIEW

Jackson County is the economic core of the 31st-largest U.S. metro (2.25M), anchored by Kansas City, Missouri — a diversified, federal-government-hardened economy with a renter-majority urban core and a housing market that is affordable at the county median but structurally broken for its renter population.

| Metric | Value |
|--------|-------|
| Median Household Income (2024) | **$68,577** (ACS 2020–2024); $67,178 prior year |
| Per Capita Income | **$39,559** |
| Unemployment (May 2026) | **3.5%** (annual 2025: 4.2%) |
| Poverty Rate | **14.1–14.3%** (vs. national 12.5%) |
| Labor Force Participation | **66.8%** |
| Median Home Value | **$230,500** — 31% below national ($332,700) |
| Cost of Living | Below national average; housing is the affordability pressure point |

**Growth signal:** MHI grew +2.1% 2023→2024. Employment grew +3.5% 2022→2023 to 360,017 workers. The economy is stable and diversified — but income growth is not keeping pace with rent escalation in the core.

**Key employers:** The **federal government is the largest employer** — 146 agencies, including the IRS Service Center (2,700–4,000 staff, one of only two paper-return processing sites), the Honeywell Kansas City Plant (2,700; produces 85% of non-nuclear components of the U.S. nuclear arsenal), and the Social Security Administration (1,700). Health care is the largest industry sector (55,465 workers), followed by retail (37,931) and manufacturing (32,663). The Stowers Institute for Medical Research, UMKC, Children's Mercy, and the Animal Health Corridor (world's largest, KC–Manhattan KS) round out the knowledge base.

**Earning vs. living gap:** The county's median income buys more house than in coastal markets ($230,500 median home vs. $750,000 in Whatcom) — **but the renter population is where the gap lives.** Median renter household income is $45,021, and median gross rent ($1,197 ACS; $1,300–$1,615 market) consumes **30.3% of renter income** — the exact threshold where cost-burden begins. 15% of the county's population lives with severe housing problems.

**The structural signal — 18,000 vacant, off-market homes:** Kansas City has over 18,000 housing units that are vacant but not for sale, not for rent, and not seasonal — **60% of all vacant units.** They are concentrated in Council Districts 3, 4, and 5 (the east side), correlate directly with blight-related 311 complaints, and represent the single largest untapped inventory in the Veritas pipeline. This is the *structural dysfunction* profile — not the acute shortage of Whatcom, not the pure vacancy of Craig County, but a **renter-majority core sitting next to an abandoned housing stock.**

**Supply side:** 344,038 units for 733K people. Building continues but is concentrated at the luxury/amenity tier (Volker rents $2,100+, Lee's Summit homes $477K). Rental inventory actually contracted (−29.8% YoY in listings) while rents rose +7.3% — the affordable tier is shrinking.

## 4. MEDIAN INCOME ANALYSIS

**The county-level affordability math closes — the renter math does not.**

| Income Tier | Households | Homeownership Reach | vs. Median Home ($230.5K–$270K) |
|------------|------------|-------------------|-------------------|
| County MHI ($68,577) | — | ~$301,000 (10% down) | **No gap — affordable** |
| Renter MHI ($45,021) | 40%+ of households | ~$198,000 | **Gap: $32,000–$72,000** |
| Very Low Income (50% AMI, family of 4) | ~$55,700 | ~$244,000 | Marginal |

**Calculation (county MHI, standard underwriting, 30-yr @ ~6.5%):**

```
Monthly payment ceiling = $68,577 × 0.30 ÷ 12 = $1,714.43
Max mortgage (6.5%, 30-yr) ≈ $1,714.43 ÷ 0.00632 ≈ $271,300
Max home price (10% down) = $271,300 ÷ 0.90 ≈ $301,400
Actual median home = $230,500 (ACS) / $260–270K (market)
→ NO AFFORDABILITY GAP at county MHI
```

**The real gap is tenure, not price.** A renter household earning the median renter income ($45,021) can support ~$198,000 of mortgage — below the $230K median home and **$70,000+ below the market median**. Renter households cannot convert to ownership without subsidy. Meanwhile the 30.3% rent-to-income ratio means renters are already at the cost-burden threshold just staying put.

**Rent burden:** Median gross rent of $1,197 consumes ~31.9% of median renter household income — cost-burdened by definition. Market rents ($1,300–$1,615) push core renters to 35–43% of income. This is the demographic that an MCF/CLT ownership vehicle would serve.

## 5. WORKFORCE SHORTAGES

| Metric | Value |
|--------|-------|
| Homeless (Jackson County PIT, Jan 2024) | **1,978** — up from 1,776 (2023), +11.4% YoY |
| KC city homeless estimate (2022) | ~3,000 |
| Winter shelter beds (city) | ~400 (100 more below 0°F) |
| Renter households cost-burdened (≥30%) | ~30.3% median GRAPI |
| Severe housing problems (county) | 15% of population |

**Who is being priced out:** The PIT count rose 11.4% year-over-year — the fastest-growing homeless population in the Veritas pipeline counties. Kansas City's Zero KC initiative has a five-pillar plan, and the city's first year-round low-barrier shelter (ARPA-funded, opened 2025) is a step forward, but supply lags demand: 1,978 counted homeless against ~400–500 winter beds.

**Sector-specific shortages:** Health care (55K workers) and federal agencies are hiring; the KC Tenants Bill of Rights and the city-funded Right to Counsel program (KCEDP, with Legal Aid of Western Missouri) signal a city actively managing displacement risk. The workforce problem in Jackson County is not employment — it is **housing stability for the employed poor**: the median renter works, but 30%+ of income goes to rent in a market where ownership is just out of reach without a subsidy.

The infrastructure play is *conversion* — land banking, rehab, and CLT insertion into the east side.

## 7. POLITICAL & COMMUNITY SENTIMENT

**Jackson County is the most reliably Democratic county in Missouri** (last Republican presidential winner: Nixon 1972; Obama carried KC 3:1). The political environment is the inverse of Craig County — but faith framing still lands, for different reasons.

| Signal | Value |
|--------|-------|
| County political lean | Solid Democratic (KC core 3:1) |
| Tenant protections | **KC Tenants Bill of Rights (2019)** — source-of-income protection, just-cause eviction requirements, anti-retaliation, Office of Tenant Advocate |
| Right to Counsel | **KCEDP** — city-funded free legal defense for evictions (with Legal Aid of Western Missouri) — one of the strongest in Missouri |
| State environment | Missouri legislature periodically considers preemption of local tenant protections — **monitor** |
| Faith landscape | Northern edge of the Bible Belt — Catholic 19.5%, Southern Baptist 18.0%, non-denominational evangelical 11.5% (Jackson County) |
| Community anger vectors | Displacement in gentrifying zips, vacancy/blight on the east side, eviction (filings down 24% YoY but 10,111/yr), racial equity (Troost-line legacy) |

**Municipal posture:** The city has a funded KC Housing Trust Fund, opened its first year-round low-barrier shelter, and administers a Tenants Bill of Rights with active enforcement. City-level political will for affordable housing is **strong and demonstrated** — the mirror of Whatcom's 7-0 council votes, but expressed through tenant protection and trust-fund policy rather than density mandates. Missouri has no HB 1110 equivalent — there is no state mandate forcing the issue.

## WHY THIS COUNTY MATTERS NOW

**Four windows are open simultaneously:**

1. **The vacancy window:** 18,000 off-market vacant units are at their cheapest acquisition basis in a decade; 26.7% of listings carry price drops — the market is softening at exactly the tier Veritas buys.

2. **The partner window:** KCCLT, Central Bank, AG/KC, and PEDC are all active but operate in silos. An MCF entrant that assembles them captures the integration premium — there is no consolidated CLT+MCF vehicle in the metro today.

3. **The policy window:** The city has demonstrated political will (Tenant Bill of Rights, Trust Fund, Right to Counsel) but has no state mandate behind it — the window is **city-led and will persist only while the current council coalition holds.**

4. **The demand window:** HAKC's HCV waitlist just closed (Mar 2026); PBV closed (May 2026); 1,978 counted homeless, up 11.4% YoY. Demand is real, documented, and growing — while the affordable rental tier is shrinking (−29.8% listings YoY).

**The crisis is local:** 60% of vacant homes are off-market — locked out of the housing system entirely. Every dollar of intervention converts a blighted liability into a permanently affordable asset.

## RECOMMENDED NARRATIVES

### For Family Offices

> **Kansas City has 18,000 homes that no one lives in — and 2,000 people with nowhere to sleep. The answer to both is the same: permanently affordable ownership.**

Jackson County, Missouri has a median home price of $230,500 — affordable at the county median income. But 60% of its vacant housing is off the market entirely, and its renter households — earning $45,021 — cannot convert to ownership without help. The city has already declared its intent: a Tenants Bill of Rights, a funded Right to Counsel, a Housing Trust Fund, and the first year-round low-barrier shelter in its history.

We are structuring a capital vehicle with the Kansas City Community Land Trust and a certified CDFI bank to acquire that vacant stock, rehabilitate it, and place it in permanent, resale-restricted ownership for the city's workforce. The CLT model keeps homes affordable across every future resale — a structural guarantee, not an expiring subsidy.

The investment is secured by:
- A $94K–$150K per-unit acquisition basis — capital efficiency unmatched in the pipeline
- A ready partner stack: KCCLT, Central Bank of KC, Abundant Ground, PEDC, Habitat
- Pay-as-we-sell financing that repays as homes close — not as they sit
- A federal-anchored economy that will not disappear
- Faith-aligned impact that is measurable, reportable, and permanent

*This is community development capital that stays in the community, works for the community, and does not leave.*

### For County Officials

> **Jackson County can convert its greatest liability — 18,000 vacant homes — into its greatest asset: permanently affordable workforce ownership. The model is proven, the partners are ready, and the demand is documented.**

The data is clear: 18,000 vacant off-market homes, 1,978 people experiencing homelessness (up 11.4% in one year), renter households at 30%+ of income on housing, and a Housing Choice Voucher waitlist that had to close because demand overwhelmed supply. The city has already spent political capital on tenant protection — the next step is ownership conversion.

Kansas City Community Land Trust, Central Bank of Kansas City, Abundant Ground, and PEDC are all active today, separately. What they lack is the construction capital layer that turns grant commitments into rehabilitated, owner-occupied homes: patient, senior-secured, with repayment aligned to unit closings.

This is not a request for subsidy. It is a request for partnership. Veritas Developments provides the capital. The county's role: accelerate land-bank disposition to CLT buyers, co-invest Housing Trust Fund dollars, and keep the permitting path open for affordable ownership product.

> **A vacant house on the east side is not a liability — it is the most efficient unit of social equity Kansas City has.**

If you own vacant property in Kansas City, you hold inventory the city's own data shows is the core problem: 18,000 units off the market, driving blight, crime, and suppressed values. You also hold the single cheapest acquisition basis in any major U.S. metro.

Options include:
